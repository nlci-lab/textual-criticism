An apparatus entry tells you which manuscripts support which reading. It does not tell you which reading is right. For that, you weigh two kinds of evidence: the witnesses themselves, and the wording.

External evidence

Who says it?

The witnesses: manuscripts, early translations, quotations.

Internal evidence

What does it say?

The wording: what a scribe would do, and what the author would write.

External evidence: the witnesses

A witness is any source for the text. It may be a Greek manuscript, an early translation (a version), or a quotation in a church writer (a father). Before looking at the words, editors ask these questions about the sources.

  1. How old? Look at the earliest witness for each reading. An older copy probably stands fewer copying steps from the original.
  2. From where? Which group of manuscripts does the witness belong to? Codex Bezae (D 05), for example, often adds words that explain.
  3. How careful? Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus were copied very carefully.
  4. How widespread? When witnesses from Egypt, Syria and Italy agree, one local mistake can hardly explain it.
  5. Independent? Or was one copied from another?
  6. How many? This comes last.

Why we do not simply count

Most Greek manuscripts are late, and many belong to the Byzantine group (cited in UBS as Byz). If five hundred late copies all go back to one ancestor, they are really one witness repeated five hundred times. Two early, independent copies can outweigh them.

Internal evidence: the words

Here you look at the readings themselves, and ask two different questions.

Transcriptional

What was a scribe likely to do?

Scribes slipped with eye and hand. They also explained hard wording, smoothed grammar, and made passages match.

Intrinsic

What was the author likely to write?

Does the reading fit this writer's words, style and thought?

Rules of thumb

The rule behind the rules

Prefer the reading that best explains the others.

If reading A easily explains how B arose, but B does not explain A, prefer A.

The harder reading

Scribes removed difficulties more often than they created them.

Limit A reading can be hard because it is a plain mistake.

The shorter reading

Scribes added explaining words more often than they removed words.

Limit Scribes also dropped words by accident. Never use this rule alone.

Harmonization

When a reading makes one passage match another, it is often the later one.

The author's style

Prefer the reading that fits the author's usual words and ideas.

Limit A scribe can also “correct” toward the author's style.

A worked example: James 2:20

Some Bibles say faith without works is “useless”. Others say “dead”. The UBS text reads ἀργή and rates it {B}.

ReadingSome witnessesEarliest
ἀργή “useless”UBS textB, C*, 1739, some Latin, Sahidic Coptic4th c.
νεκρά “dead”א, A, C², Byz, Syriac4th c.
κενή “empty”𝔓⁷⁴, one Latin manuscript7th c.
  • Verses 17 and 26 both say “dead”. A scribe could easily write it again in verse 20; that is harmonization.
  • ἀργή is built from “not” + ἔργον (“work”) and stands next to ἔργων: an author's play on words that a scribe flattens.
  • Codex C was first written with “useless” and later corrected to “dead”.

So for a translator the choice changes little in meaning. If your readers know “dead”, you can follow the UBS text and still respect their Bible.
